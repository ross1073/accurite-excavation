A new road can look perfect the day it's paved and still be a failure. Two winters later there are dips where there shouldn't be dips, cracks running with the centerline, and low spots that hold water every spring.

Almost none of that is a paving problem. It's a problem with what was left underneath.

The work that decides whether a road holds its shape happens weeks before anyone brings asphalt to the site — during site clearing and grading, when the organic material either comes off the alignment or doesn't.

## Why Grass Cannot Support a Road

Native ground is not a uniform material. The top layer of a field or pasture is topsoil laced with roots, grass, and decaying plant matter. It's excellent for growing things and terrible for holding weight.

Two things go wrong when you build a road on top of it.

**It decomposes.** Organic material is not stable. Buried under a roadway, grass and root mass break down over months and years, and as they break down the volume they occupied disappears. The surface above settles into that void. This is the classic delayed failure — the road performs fine through the first season, then develops dips as decomposition catches up with it.

**It won't compact.** Compaction works by pressing soil particles into tighter contact with each other and driving out air and water. Springy, fibrous, root-bound material resists that. You can run a roller over it all day and still not reach the density a roadway subgrade requires, because the material itself isn't capable of it.

The engineering term for material that has to come out is *deleterious* — anything in the subgrade that will weaken it or change volume over time. Topsoil, roots, sod, buried wood, old debris, soft saturated pockets. Whatever it is, it can't stay under the road.

## Clearing vs. Grubbing

These get used interchangeably in conversation, and they're not the same operation.

**Clearing** is everything above the ground line — brush, vegetation, and growth removed off the surface of the alignment.

**Grubbing** is what's below it. The dozer strips off the root mat and the organic topsoil layer, taking the ground down past the growing zone to material that's clean enough to build on. This is the step that actually protects the road. It also happens out of sight and adds cost up front, which makes it the easiest line for a bid to leave out — so it's worth confirming it's in there.

The material that comes off doesn't disappear. It gets pushed into windrows, stockpiled for reuse as landscape topsoil elsewhere on the property, or [hauled off site](/services/hauling-delivery). On a [subdivision or commercial site](/services/commercial-projects), or down a long driveway alignment, that can be a substantial volume of dirt with somewhere to be.

## Establishing Strip Depth

There's no universal number. The right depth depends on what's actually under your site, which is why it can't be settled from a desk.

The strip depth comes from the project's geotechnical report and the road's design section. A soils engineer takes borings, identifies where the organic layer ends and competent native material begins, and the spec sets the depth from there. On some sites that boundary is shallow and consistent. On others — old irrigated farm ground, filled areas, anywhere with a history nobody documented — it moves around across the same parcel.

That variability is exactly the problem. Strip too shallow in one section and you've left organic material under the road. Strip too deep across the whole alignment and now you're buying imported structural fill to bring the grade back up, which is expensive dirt to move twice.

The goal isn't "deep." The goal is *right, consistently, across the entire alignment.*

## The Value of GPS Grade Control

That consistency problem is what machine control solves, and it's why AccuRite's dozer runs a GPS grade control system. It's the machine in the video at the top of this post — the two masts standing up off the blade are its GPS antennas.

The road's design surface is loaded into the machine as a three-dimensional model. GPS positioning tracks where the blade is in real space and compares it to where the model says the finished surface should be. The operator works to a live display of cut and fill instead of working to stakes and a best guess between them.

The footage shows what that produces on the ground: a clean, consistent strip running the length of the alignment, the sod and topsoil rolled off the blade and windrowed to the side, and green pasture right up against the edge of the cut.

On a job like stripping a road alignment, that changes the work in a few specific ways:

- **The strip depth is uniform end to end.** Every pass is referenced to the same design surface, not to how the last pass looked or where the nearest stake is.
- **The crew isn't waiting on survey.** Conventional grading is a loop of cut, stop, shoot grade, check, adjust. Machine control collapses that loop, so the dozer keeps working.
- **Less overcut.** Material removed below the design surface has to be replaced with engineered fill and compacted back. Cutting accurately the first time is cheaper than cutting deep and buying the difference back.
- **The finished subgrade matches the plan.** Elevations and cross-slope come out where the design put them, which matters as soon as drainage is involved — and on a road, drainage is always involved.

There's nothing exotic about the technology. It's standard on serious earthwork now. What matters is whether the machine doing your dirt work has it — a fair question to ask anyone bidding your site, because it's the difference between grade held by measurement and grade held by eye.

## Verifying Subgrade Stability

Clean subgrade isn't finished subgrade. Once the organic material is gone, the exposed surface has to be brought to grade and compacted to the density the design calls for.

That's typically verified two ways. **Proof rolling** runs a loaded truck or heavy roller across the subgrade while someone watches for movement — soft or pumping areas show themselves under load, get excavated out, and get replaced with compacted structural fill. **Density testing** by an independent lab confirms the compaction numerically against a Proctor standard.

Only after subgrade passes does base course go down. Everything above that point — base, asphalt, curb — is only as good as the surface it was built on. We've written more about what makes ground stable enough to build on in [soil stabilization and why it matters](/blog/soil-stabilization-importance).

## 4 Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Contractor

If you're a builder, developer, or property owner about to put in a road, a long driveway, or a building pad, four questions will tell you a lot about the bid in front of you. (For the broader version of this conversation, see [how to choose an excavation contractor in Utah](/blog/how-to-choose-excavation-contractor-utah).)

1. **Is stripping and grubbing included in the base bid?** If it comes back later as a change order, the number you're comparing isn't the number you'll pay.
2. **How was the strip depth determined?** It should reference the soils report or the plan set, because that's the only place the real answer lives.
3. **What is the plan for managing stripped topsoil?** Stockpiled on site, spread somewhere useful, or hauled — it needs a destination and a cost attached to it.
4. **How will the crew maintain accurate grade?** Stakes and a grade checker can absolutely produce a good road. GPS machine control does it more consistently and with less rework.
