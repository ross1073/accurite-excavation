An El Niño winter is making national headlines, but that does not tell you how much water your Northern Utah property will receive. NOAA’s outlook leaves Weber, Davis, Box Elder and Morgan counties at equal chances of wetter, near-normal or drier conditions. The practical move is to check where water goes on your property and fix drainage problems while the ground allows.

For a homeowner, that means looking beyond the puddle. For an acreage owner, it means following the whole route: sloped ground, roadside ditch, driveway culvert and the outlet beyond it. Water needs a connected path, not just somewhere to collect.

## What NOAA actually says about winter 2026–27

In its [November 19 seasonal outlook discussion](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/fxus05.html), NOAA says a strong El Niño is underway and forecast to persist through January–March 2027. NOAA expects it to be a major influence on national winter weather.

Nationally, NOAA’s outlook favors wetter conditions across the southern United States and warmer conditions across the northern United States. But those broad patterns are not a forecast for every property within those regions.

Here is the local distinction:

- **December–February precipitation:** NOAA gives Northern Utah equal chances of below-normal, near-normal or above-normal precipitation.
- **December–February temperature:** Northern Utah also has equal chances across the three temperature categories.
- **January–March temperature:** NOAA shows a slight tilt toward above-normal temperatures, in the 33–40% probability band.
- **January–March precipitation:** Northern Utah remains in equal chances.

Those details come from NOAA’s [December–February precipitation map](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/lead01/off01_prcp.gif), [December–February temperature map](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/lead01/off01_temp.gif), [January–March temperature map](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/lead02/off02_temp.gif) and [January–March precipitation map](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/lead02/off02_prcp.gif).

“Equal chances” is a three-way outlook, not a prediction of an average winter. NOAA is not favoring a wet or dry Northern Utah winter. Its [October 15 Winter Outlook](https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/el-nino-looms-large-over-us-winter-outlook) also explains that snowfall is generally not predictable more than a week ahead.

## What a warm spell could change on your property

These are physical possibilities, not NOAA forecasts for Northern Utah.

If winter runs warm, precipitation that could have fallen as snow can arrive as rain instead. That water reaches the ground immediately rather than remaining stored as snow. Warm spells can also mean more freeze-thaw cycles, which can work on gravel surfaces, culvert ends, soil and retaining-wall backfill. A warm late winter could bring earlier snowmelt and runoff.

None of that means an El Niño winter guarantees drainage trouble. It means drainage should work whenever water arrives.

Northern Utah clay soils drain slowly. Where surface grading or buried drainage leaves water without an outlet, it can collect against foundations and retaining walls. Our post on [How to Avoid Flooding in Your New Utah Home](/blog/avoid-flooding-new-home) explains why exterior drainage deserves attention from the start.

## Before: check the whole drainage route

Walk the property when footing is safe. Start near buildings, then follow the ground downhill to the final discharge point. Do not enter a ditch, culvert or flowing water to inspect it.

### Check grading around the house

Look for soil that slopes toward the foundation, low spots beside exterior walls and landscaping that blocks an existing drainage path.

The goal is a continuous surface route away from the house—not simply moving a puddle several feet over. Avoid piling soil against siding or covering foundation openings. Where the ground needs reshaping, [grading and land clearing](/services/grading-land-clearing) includes drainage regrading to establish that route.

### Check ditches and driveway culverts

A ditch needs both an open channel and a shape that carries water. Look for sediment, vegetation, slumped sides and places where equipment or vehicles have flattened the channel.

At each driveway culvert, check:

- Whether the inlet and outlet are visible and open.
- Whether either end is crushed, separated or undermined.
- Whether sediment has reduced the opening.
- Whether the ditch continues beyond the outlet.

An open pipe cannot solve a blocked downstream ditch. A damaged or poorly positioned culvert may need excavation and replacement rather than another round of debris removal.

### Check gravel driveways and access roads

Look for wheel ruts, low shoulders and flat stretches that hold water. A gravel road needs an appropriate crown or cross-slope, with drainage that moves water off the traveled surface.

Adding gravel alone does not correct every drainage problem. Reshaping the road, restoring side ditches and hauling suitable gravel or fill may all be part of the repair. Check where the road sheds water so the correction does not create erosion on the next slope.

### Check retaining-wall drainage

Inspect visible outlets for blockage and look for settled ground behind the wall. Do not dig into the wall’s base or remove supporting material to investigate.

Drainage belongs behind the wall as well as in front of it. AccuRite’s [rock walls and retaining walls](/services/rock-walls-retaining-walls) service includes drainage behind every wall, using perforated pipe in gravel. That drainage still needs a working discharge route.

### Follow downspouts and buried drains to an outlet

Check exterior downspout outlets, yard drains and area-drain grates. Make sure landscaping, sediment or stored materials have not blocked them.

For buried lines, ask a basic question: **Where does this pipe discharge?**

A storm drain or French drain needs a suitable outlet. If the route is unknown, disconnected or ends in a low spot, adding another inlet may not help. ## After: inspect what the water left behind

After a big rain, rain-on-snow event or fast melt, wait until conditions are safe before walking the property. Take photos from stable ground. Note where water entered, where it collected and where it left.

### Pooling against the foundation or settled backfill

Look for standing water beside exterior foundation walls and new depressions along foundation backfill or utility trenches.

Persistent pooling, reverse slopes and settled trenches are reasons to have an excavation contractor evaluate the outside grading and drainage. Filling a depression without checking the water route may leave the underlying problem in place.

### Washouts and ruts on driveways or access roads

Check for channels cut into gravel, exposed pipe, soft shoulders and washed-out road edges. Keep vehicles off undermined sections.

These problems may need excavation, regrading and replacement gravel or fill. The repair should address where water crossed or followed the road, not just smooth the surface.

### An overtopped culvert or sediment-filled ditch

Look for debris caught above the culvert opening, material deposited across the driveway and erosion around the pipe ends. Those signs can help identify where water backed up or escaped.

A plugged opening may need clearing. A crushed pipe, displaced culvert, eroded crossing or ditch that has lost its shape needs a closer excavation assessment. Do not assume one overtopping event proves the pipe needs to be larger; blockage, alignment and the downstream outlet also matter.

### A leaning wall, muddy seepage or eroded slope

A retaining wall that newly leans, bulges or weeps mud needs assessment. Stay clear of the affected area and avoid adding loads above it. Excavation and drainage work may be needed, and some wall conditions may also require engineering review.

On slopes, look for fresh channels, exposed roots and soil deposited below. Regrading or hauling in replacement material may help, but first identify the concentrated water source. Otherwise, the same route remains available for another washout.

## Prepare for the water path, not the headline

The useful takeaway from the El Niño winter outlook is not to assume a wet season—or a dry one. It is to make sure your grading, ditches, culverts and buried drains work together.
